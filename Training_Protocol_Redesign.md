## ⚠ Read first — the neurological situation

You have now had **two separate neurological episodes**: four months of numbness on the right leg that resolved on its own, and an acute simultaneous right perioral and right little-finger event during heavy training.

The C8-and-trigeminal explanation is anatomically coherent for the second event. **It does not account for the first.** Mechanical compression from lifting resolves in hours to days, as your finger did — it does not usually produce four months of numbness and then clear. Two events separated in time and body region is the pattern that triggers a neurological workup rather than form coaching.

**On TRT this matters more, not less.** Testosterone therapy raises haematocrit; raised haematocrit raises blood viscosity and thrombotic risk. Acute one-sided facial numbness in a man on TRT who lifts heavily is a scenario where stroke and TIA need excluding.

**Ask your doctor for:** haematocrit and haemoglobin, blood pressure, and a neurological examination. Mention the four-month leg episode explicitly — that is the detail that changes what they look for.

**Emergency signs — stop and seek care immediately:** numbness spreading to face, tongue or arm; facial drooping; slurred speech or trouble swallowing; sudden weakness in an arm or leg; loss of balance; sudden severe headache; any change in vision.

Combined with a strained neck position and tight scalenes, that is the most plausible mechanical route to irritating both the C8 root and the descending trigeminal tract at the same instant. You still need to brace — you need to brace **while exhaling** rather than against a closed throat.

## TL;DR

- **Six slots, run in order:** `01 Lower A → 02 Upper A → 03 Zone 2 → 04 Lower B → 05 Upper B → 06 VO₂max (or Zone 2)` → back to 01.
- **No weekdays, no week numbers, no phases.** Finish a slot, rest as long as you want, start the next. Because rest only ever pushes the cycle further apart, spacing between same-muscle sessions only ever gets longer — you cannot break the recovery rules by resting. Aim to close a cycle in **6–8 days**; beyond that, every muscle drops below twice weekly and the volume figures here stop being true.
- **Load moves on conditions, not on a calendar.** Add weight only when every condition in §5 is met. The HSR tendon track still runs 15RM → 6RM, but it gets there when the 24-hour rule keeps clearing, not by a fixed schedule.
- **The 4×4 is included, and substituting Zone 2 is always a valid choice** — not a compromise. Under the updated Zone 2 evidence, Zone 2 earns its place on recovery-cost grounds rather than mitochondrial ones, which is exactly why it makes a good substitute when you are beaten up or uncleared.
- **Clearances needed:** haematocrit, BP, lipids and PSA with your prescriber; explicit clearance for maximal-HR intervals; a neurological assessment for the symptoms above; and a confirmed knee diagnosis from a physiotherapist. This plan assumes patellar tendinopathy — see §8.1.

## 1. The cycle

| Slot | Session | Type | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **01** | **Lower A** | Resistance | Quad/knee focus, primary HSR tendon load |
| **02** | **Upper A** | Resistance | Horizontal push/pull, arms, forearms |
| **03** | **Zone 2** | Conditioning | Standalone aerobic, lowest recovery cost |
| **04** | **Lower B** | Resistance | Hip/hamstring focus, second tendon dose |
| **05** | **Upper B** | Resistance | Vertical push/pull, arms, forearms |
| **06** | **VO₂max 4×4** *(or Zone 2)* | Conditioning | Intervals — substitute Zone 2 freely |

Then back to 01.

**Why this order.** If you run the slots on consecutive days you get 72 h between Lower A and Lower B and between Upper A and Upper B; Zone 2 falls between the two lower slots as active recovery; and the intervals land two slots after the last heavy lower day. Insert rest days anywhere and every one of those gaps only gets longer. The order is self-correcting — the sequence protects spacing without you having to think about the calendar.

**What it does not protect is rate.** Six slots across 6–8 days keeps every muscle at roughly twice weekly, which is what the volume table in §3 assumes. Stretch a cycle to 12 days and you are training everything about 1.2×/week, and the numbers stop being true.

**Never combine lifting and conditioning on one day.** Interference is small in the pooled data, but it concentrates in exactly your profile — lower-body maximal strength in trained males (SMD −0.43, Huiberts 2024) and mostly same-session (−0.66 same-session vs −0.10 separated, Petré 2021). Separation removes the condition where the effect appears, permanently and structurally.

## 2. The scientific position, verified

Each item below was checked against the primary source in August 2026. Where the popular framing overshoots the source, the correction is stated.

### 2.1 Volume drives hypertrophy; frequency matters more for *strength* than for size

Pelland, Remmert, Robinson, Hinson & Zourdos. *Sports Med.* 2026 Feb;56(2):481–505. doi:10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w (epub 2025 Dec 4). PMID 41343037.

- **0.24% additional hypertrophy per additional set** (95% CrI 0.15–0.33) at the average fractional weekly volume of 12.25 sets — comparable to Schoenfeld's earlier ~0.38%/set slope.
- Undetectable outcome superiority beyond **~31 fractional weekly sets** for hypertrophy; beyond **~3 fractional weekly sets** and a fractional frequency of 3 for strength.
- **Correction to the brief:** the brief says "frequency secondary." The paper is more specific — the volume–hypertrophy and volume–strength dose-responses differ, and **frequency shows consistently identifiable effects for strength but not for hypertrophy**. So 2×/week is fully sufficient for size; for maximal strength, the 2×/week frequency in this program is at the useful end of the curve rather than merely adequate. That is an argument *for* this split, not against it.

Per-session ceiling: Remmert, Pelland, Robinson, Hinson & Zourdos (2025), *SportRxiv* preprint — point of undetectable outcome superiority ≈ **11 fractional sets per session** for hypertrophy, ≈2 direct sets per session for strength. **Caveat: this is a preprint, not peer-reviewed**, and high per-session volumes were underrepresented (mean 5.95 ± 4.49 fractional sets). Treat ~11 as a soft ceiling per muscle per session, not a law.

Frequency support: Schoenfeld, Grgic & Krieger. *J Sports Sci.* 2019;37(11):1286–1295 — 2×/week is sufficient when weekly volume is equated.

### 2.2 Proximity to failure: 1–3 RIR captures nearly everything

Refalo, Helms, Trexler, Hamilton & Fyfe. *Sports Med.* 2023;53(3):649–665 — hypertrophy is not meaningfully enhanced by training to momentary failure over stopping short.
Robinson, Pelland, Remmert, Refalo, Jukic, Steele & Zourdos. *Sports Med.* 2024;54(9):2209–2231. doi:10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2 — dose–response meta-regressions on estimated proximity to failure.

Practical read: **1–3 RIR on compounds, 0–2 RIR on isolation.** Maximal strength is comparatively insensitive to proximity to failure, which is convenient — it means you can leave a rep in the tank on heavy lower-body work without paying for it, which is exactly what a symptomatic tendon needs.

### 2.3 Knee rehab: HSR is the core, and nothing has beaten it

**Liu Y, et al.** Comparative effectiveness of exercise interventions for patellar tendinopathy: a systematic review and network meta-analysis of RCTs. *BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil.* 2026 May 26. doi:10.1186/s13102-026-01743-4. PMID 42192475.
- 17 RCTs. **No exercise intervention showed clear superiority over HSR** for short-term VISA-P improvement.
- **Correction to the brief:** the brief states eccentric-only was "frequently weakest." In *this* NMA, **eccentric overload had the most favourable point estimate versus HSR** (SMD 0.33, 95% CI −0.87 to 1.52 — confidence interval crosses zero, so not statistically distinguishable), followed by blood-flow-restriction training (0.26) and moderate slow resistance (0.12). Flywheel, eccentric *step* training and concentric-only estimated less favourably. The authors explicitly caution that probability rankings were exploratory and should not be read as evidence of meaningful superiority.
- The "eccentric is worst" finding comes from a **different** paper: Li Y, et al. *Heliyon.* 2024;10(21):e39171. doi:10.1016/j.heliyon.2024.e39171 — 7 studies, eccentric training ranked worst for VISA-P, moderate slow resistance best. Small network, low confidence.
- **Honest synthesis:** HSR is the correct default because it is never beaten and it integrates into real strength training. It is not proven *superior* to several alternatives. Confidence intervals across this literature are wide.

**Kongsgaard M, et al.** *Scand J Med Sci Sports.* 2009 Dec;19(6):790–802. PMID 19793213. 39 male patients randomised to corticosteroid injection vs eccentric decline squat vs HSR for 12 weeks.
- All three improved VISA-p and VAS at 12 weeks; **improvements were maintained at 6 months in eccentric and HSR but deteriorated in the corticosteroid group.**
- HSR reduced tendon swelling (−12 ± 13%) and vascularisation (−45 ± 23%) and produced **elevated collagen network turnover**.
- **6-month treatment satisfaction differed significantly between groups, highest in HSR.** (The widely circulated "70% vs 22%" figures come from the results section; the abstract reports only that satisfaction differed. I have not independently verified the exact percentages, so the claim here is limited to what is verifiable.)
- **Important honest note the brief omits:** *tendon mechanical properties were unaffected by treatment.* Twelve weeks of HSR improved symptoms and turnover without measurably changing tendon mechanics. Symptom improvement is not the same as structural repair.

**Isometrics — real, but oversold.**
- Rio E, et al. *Br J Sports Med.* 2015 Oct;49(19):1277–83. doi:10.1136/bjsports-2014-094386. PMID 25979840 — **n = 6 volleyball players.** Single-session crossover; isometric produced greater immediate analgesia than isotonic.
- Clifford C, Challoumas D, Paul L, Syme G, Millar NL. *BMJ Open Sport Exerc Med.* 2020;6(1):e000760. doi:10.1136/bmjsem-2020-000760. PMC7406028 — 10 studies (3 good quality, 7 poor). **Based on limited (level 3) evidence, isometric exercise was NOT superior to isotonic exercise for chronic tendinopathy, either immediately post-treatment or in the short term (≤12 weeks), on any outcome measured.**
- Challoumas D, et al. *BMJ Open Sport Exerc Med.* 2021 NMA — isometric ≈ isotonic for immediate post-intervention pain relief (MD −1.03, 95% CI −2.6 to 0.5, p = 0.19).
- **Verdict: optional pain tool. Use it if it works for you on the day. It is not a required stage and it is not a magic bullet.** The 5 × 45 s @ ~70% MVIC dosing is correctly transcribed from Rio.

### 2.4 Concurrent training: the interference that survives is *your* interference

- **Schumann M, Feuerbacher JF, Sünkeler M, Freitag N, Rønnestad BR, Doma K, Lundberg TR.** *Sports Med.* 2022;52(3):601–612. doi:10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7. 43 studies. SMDs: maximal strength **−0.06**, hypertrophy **−0.01**, explosive strength **−0.28** (p = 0.007). Interference was independent of aerobic modality (cycling vs running), frequency, training status, age (<40 vs >40) and training modality. **Explosive strength attenuation was exacerbated by same-session training.**
- **Huiberts RO, Wüst RCI, van der Zwaard S.** *Sports Med.* 2024 Feb;54(2):485–503. doi:10.1007/s40279-023-01943-9. PMID 37847373. 59 studies, 1,346 participants. **Lower-body strength adaptations blunted in males (SMD −0.43, 95% CI −0.64 to −0.22) but not females (0.08); group difference p = 0.03.** No sex difference for upper-body strength or power.
- **Petré H, et al.** *Sports Med.* 2021 — interference on maximal strength appeared in trained individuals (ES −0.35), and was concentrated in same-session pairings (ES −0.66) versus separated sessions (ES −0.10).
- **Gao J & Yu L.** Effects of concurrent training sequence on VO₂max and lower-limb strength performance. *Front Physiol.* 2023. doi:10.3389/fphys.2023.1192593. PMID 36968888 — when same-session is unavoidable, **lift before cardio** to protect lower-body strength.

**What this means for you specifically.** The popular summary — "interference only affects explosive power" — is true of the Schumann pooled estimate but incomplete. Stratify by sex and region and a small, real lower-body strength effect appears in males, and it is concentrated where sessions are combined. You are a trained male whose two headline goals are maximal strength and hypertrophy, with lower body carrying the rehab load. **Full separation is doing real work here.** The ≥6 h rule and the lift-before-cardio rule are retained only as fallbacks for weeks when the schedule breaks.

### 2.5 Zone 2: demoted, not deleted

**Storoschuk KL, Moran-MacDonald A, Gibala MJ, Gurd BJ.** Much Ado About Zone 2. *Sports Med.* 2025 Jul;55(7):1611–1624. doi:10.1007/s40279-025-02261-y. PMID 40560504.

> "We conclude that current evidence does not support Zone 2 training as the optimal intensity for improving mitochondrial or fatty acid oxidative capacity. Further, evidence suggests prioritizing higher exercise intensities (> Zone 2) is critical to maximize cardiometabolic health benefits, particularly in the context of lower training volumes."

Two things worth holding onto:
- This is a **narrative review**, not a meta-analysis. It is an argument built on 167 sources, and it is a good argument, but it is a review-level claim.
- The mechanistic core: mitochondrial adaptation appears to respond most robustly **above ~65% of work-rate maximum** (Granada et al. 2018 meta-analysis, cited within), and Zone 2 defined at <2 mmol/L lactate sits below that threshold for many people. The Zone 2 case rested largely on observational data from elite endurance athletes doing very large volumes — which is not your situation.

**Honest framing for your Zone 2 day.** It stays in the program, and it earns its slot on four grounds, none of which is "optimal mitochondrial stimulus":
1. **Lowest recovery cost per unit of aerobic work** — critical when four lifting days and a healing tendon already own your recovery budget.
2. **Active recovery** between Lower A and Lower B.
3. **Aerobic base and capillarisation**, which are real adaptations at this intensity.
4. **A way to add aerobic volume once recoverable volume at higher intensities is spent.**

This is precisely why the flexible sixth day should **default to the 4×4** whenever recovery permits, and drop to Zone 2 only when it doesn't. Under the pre-2025 framing that choice was a coin flip. Under Storoschuk it is not.

### 2.6 VO₂max and mortality: strong predictor, unproven cause

**The association is about as robust as observational epidemiology gets:**
- Mandsager K, et al. *JAMA Netw Open.* 2018;1(6):e183605 — Cleveland Clinic cohort, 122,007 patients; higher cardiorespiratory fitness associated with lower long-term mortality with no observed upper limit of benefit.
- Kodama S, et al. *JAMA.* 2009;301(19):2024–2035 — meta-analysis; each 1-MET higher fitness associated with meaningfully lower all-cause and CHD mortality.
- Lang JJ, et al. *Br J Sports Med.* 2024;58(10):556–566. PMID 38599681 — overview of meta-analyses spanning **>20.9 million observations across 199 cohort studies**; CRF is a strong and consistent predictor of morbidity and mortality. *This is the citation to use if you want the single strongest one.*
- Mayo Clin Proc. 2022 meta-analysis, 37 studies / 2.26 M participants — pooled RR 0.55 (95% CI 0.50–0.61) for all-cause mortality, top vs bottom CRF tertile.

**Correction to the brief — Lundy 2026.** Lundy L, Reilly RB & Fleming N. *Sci Rep.* 2026;16:21761. doi:10.1038/s41598-026-52475-x. This is a **cross-sectional online survey of 340 multi-marathoners** with VO₂max **estimated from wearable device data**. The paper does **not** derive a 3.7% mortality reduction per 1 mL/kg/min — it **models** mortality risk *using* that figure taken from prior meta-analytic literature. Citing it as evidence for the 3.7% figure inverts the direction of the evidence. Use Lang 2024 or Kodama 2009 instead.

**The causality caveat, verified.** Kjaergaard AD, Ellervik C, Jessen N, Lessard SJ. *J Clin Endocrinol Metab.* 2025 May;110(5):1451–1459. doi:10.1210/clinem/dgae393. PMID 38864459. Bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomisation: **"Despite being a strong predictor of mortality, VO₂max is not causally associated with T2D or longevity."** Genetically predicted VO₂max showed no association with longevity, while body fat percentage and type 2 diabetes did show independent causal effects on longevity.

Two details in that paper worth your attention specifically:
- Physical activity, appendicular lean mass and body-fat percentage all showed **causal effects on VO₂max** — i.e. training raises fitness; the arrow that fails is fitness → lifespan.
- **Hematocrit and free testosterone in men were among the "performance enhancers" causally associated with higher VO₂max.** On TRT, part of any VO₂max improvement you see may be hematologic rather than cardiac — which is the same physiology your prescriber is monitoring for risk. Do not read a rising VO₂max as unambiguously good news without a hematocrit alongside it.

**And the one big RCT.** Stensvold D, et al. Generation 100. *BMJ.* 2020;371:m3485. PMID 33028588. 1,567 adults aged 70–77, randomised to 5 years of HIIT, MICT, or national guidelines. **All-cause mortality did not differ between control and the combined training group.** HIIT showed a non-significant trend toward lower mortality and produced significantly higher CRF and quality of life. The authors note the control group trained more than anticipated and the cohort was healthier than average.

**The honest position:** improving VO₂max is rational, cheap and low-risk, and it tracks with better outcomes across enormous datasets. It is a marker at least as much as a mechanism, and the one long RCT designed to test the mortality question came back null. Train it because it makes you more capable and it is strongly associated with living longer — not because raising a number is proven to buy years.

### 2.7 Norwegian 4×4

**Helgerud J, Høydal K, Wang E, Karlsen T, Berg P, Bjerkaas M, Simonsen T, Helgesen C, Hjorth N, Bach R, Hoff J.** Aerobic high-intensity intervals improve VO₂max more than moderate training. *Med Sci Sports Exerc.* 2007;39(4):665–671. doi:10.1249/mss.0b013e3180304570. PMID 17414804.

40 moderately trained males, four groups matched for total work and frequency, 8 weeks: long slow distance (70% HRmax), lactate threshold (85% HRmax), 15/15 intervals (90–95% HRmax), and **4 × 4 min at 90–95% HRmax with 3 min active recovery at 70% HRmax.** Both high-intensity interval groups improved VO₂max significantly more than the two moderate groups; VO₂max changes tracked stroke-volume changes.

**Two honest notes:** the 15/15 protocol performed comparably to the 4×4 — **the intensity is what matters, not the specific 4-minute block.** And headline figures of "7–13%" circulate loosely; the defensible statement is that high-intensity intervals were significantly superior to volume-matched moderate training over 8 weeks.

Extended safely to heart-failure patients: Wisløff U, et al. *Circulation.* 2007;115(24):3086–3094.

### 2.8 Long muscle lengths: real, modest, and more precise than the brief states

- Partial ROM at **long** vs **short** muscle length: long lengths produced significantly greater hypertrophy — **ES 0.283 (95% CI 0.04–0.52, p = 0.036)**, 8 studies (*Sport Sci Health*, 2026. doi:10.1007/s11332-025-01586-5). Real, and modest.
- **Lengthened partials vs full ROM: Wolf et al. 2025 (*PeerJ* 13:e18904) found *similar* muscular adaptations in trained individuals.** So the correct claim is *"train at long lengths,"* not *"lengthened partials beat full ROM."* Full ROM already covers long lengths.
- **Maeo S, Wu Y, Huang M, Sakurai H, Kusagawa Y, Sugiyama T, Kanehisa H, Isaka T.** *Eur J Sport Sci.* 2023;23(7):1240–1250. doi:10.1080/17461391.2022.2100279. 21 adults, within-subject, cable elbow extensions 90–0°, 70% 1RM, 5 sets, 2×/week, 12 weeks. Long head **+28.5% overhead vs +19.6% neutral** (1.5-fold, Cohen's d = 0.61, p < 0.001); lateral+medial **+14.6% vs +10.5%** (1.4-fold, d = 0.39); whole triceps **+19.9% vs +13.9%.** Fully verified. Limitations: n = 21, single isolation movement, healthy young adults, one study.

**What survives into the program:** overhead cable triceps extension for the long head, incline curls for the biceps long head, long-length-biased leg extension, seated leg curl over lying. Selection at long lengths — not partial-ROM gimmicks.

### 2.9 Sleep — the biggest lever, with no fabricated number attached

**Do not cite "80% of adaptation occurs during slow-wave sleep."** No peer-reviewed source assigns any percentage to tissue or cardiovascular adaptation occurring during SWS. The number is a corruption of a growth-hormone statistic: **~70% of daily GH output occurs during early sleep in men** (Van Cauter E, Plat L, Copinschi G. *Sleep.* 1998;21(6):553–566), and ~70% of sleep GH pulses coincide with SWS (Van Cauter & Plat. *J Pediatr.* 1996;128(5 Pt 2):S32–7). The "80%" that genuinely appears in the literature is NREM as a share of total nightly sleep.

Even the downstream link is weak: muscle protein synthesis appears **time-dependent rather than state-dependent** (Vorster, *CISS* 2023), and cardiovascular mechanisms during SWS "remain elusive" (Huwiler et al., *Eur Heart J* 2023).

**7–9 h on a consistent schedule remains the single biggest recovery lever in this program.** It does not need a fake statistic to justify it.

### 2.10 Gastrocnemius vs soleus — supported

The biarticular gastrocnemius is most active with the knee extended (standing calf raise); knee flexion places it in active insufficiency and shifts load to the soleus, whose activity is largely unchanged across knee angles (Arampatzis et al. 2006; Signorile; Reid). **Program both.** Standing = gastroc. Seated = soleus. Both appear twice weekly in this build.

### 2.11 Pendulum / hack squat — mechanistically reasonable, not RCT-backed

Both reduce axial spinal load and balance demand and permit controlled deep knee flexion under quad-dominant load; the pendulum's arc produces a resistance profile heaviest near the stretched position. **There is no head-to-head RCT joint-load data establishing either as categorically "knee-friendly."** They are defensible, sensible choices for your knees. The marketing outruns the evidence. Judge them by your own 24-hour symptom response, not by the claim.

### 2.12 Connective-tissue supplements

- **Shaw G, Lee-Barthel A, Ross ML, Wang B, Baar K.** *Am J Clin Nutr.* 2017;105(1):136–143. doi:10.3945/ajcn.116.138594. Randomised double-blind crossover, 8 healthy men: 15 g vitamin-C-enriched gelatin ~1 h before a 6-minute rope-skipping bout roughly **doubled the amino-terminal propeptide of collagen I** in blood. Strong mechanism, tiny sample, biomarker outcome.
- **Bischof K, Moitzi AM, Stafilidis S, König D.** *Sports Med.* 2024 Nov;54(11):2865–2888. doi:10.1007/s40279-024-02079-0. PMID 39060741 — systematic review with meta-analysis of long-term collagen peptide supplementation plus training on strength, musculotendinous remodelling, recovery and body composition.
- **Kirmse M, Hein V, Schäfer R, Platen P.** *Dtsch Z Sportmed.* 2024;75:179–188. doi:10.5960/dzsm.2024.605 — 13 studies. **No significant effect on strength performance.** Kirmse's experimental work also found collagen did not increase intramuscular connective-tissue protein synthesis over one week of high-volume resistance and plyometric training versus placebo.

**Verdict: take it for the tendon, not for the lift.** It will not make you stronger.

## 4. The sessions

**Loading defaults**
- Compounds 3–4 sets · isolation 2–3 sets
- Strength emphasis: 3–6 reps @ 1–3 RIR, rest 3–5 min
- Hypertrophy: 6–15 reps @ 1–3 RIR, rest 2–3 min
- Isolation: 0–2 RIR, rest 60–120 s
- **HSR tempo: 3 s concentric / 3 s eccentric** on designated tendon movements only
- Double progression: add reps within range, then add load

**On the left knee.** Your brief asks for more volume on the left. Here is a more defensible version of that instruction, and the reason for the change:

> **Attention, not extra heavy load.** On every unilateral movement, **lead with the left** — perform the left side first, take it to the prescribed RIR, then match that rep count on the right. This makes the symptomatic side set the dose and prevents the strong side dragging the weak side into overload. Add **one extra set of low-load accessory work** (hip abduction, tibialis, terminal-range extension) to the left. ### ZONE 2 DAY — standalone *(Wed, and Sat when fatigued)*

**Protocol**
- **Duration:** 45–60 min continuous
- **Intensity:** LT1 / first ventilatory threshold — approximately 2 mmol/L blood lactate, **~60–70% HRmax**, RPE 3–4/10
- **The talk test:** you can speak in full sentences without gasping. If you can only manage short phrases, you are above Zone 2 and turning a low-cost day into a recovery cost.
- **Modality:** knee-friendly — stationary bike, rower, incline treadmill walk, elliptical. Avoid running while the tendon is symptomatic.
- **Warm-up/cool-down:** 5 min easy either end, included in the total.

**What it is for, honestly:** active recovery, aerobic base, capillarisation, lower-limb endurance, and cheap aerobic volume that costs almost nothing in recovery. **Not** a uniquely optimal mitochondrial stimulus — see §2.5.

**Aerobic threshold verification test.** Every 8 weeks, repeat the same session on the same machine: fix your heart rate at a chosen ceiling (say 135 bpm) and record distance or average power over 30 minutes. **Improvement = more distance or watts at the same heart rate.** Track it. If it stalls for two consecutive tests while the 4×4 is being run consistently, that is a signal your recovery budget is spent, not that Zone 2 has stopped working.

### NORWEGIAN 4×4 — flexible day, default *(Sat)*

**Requires explicit physician clearance before first use. See §11.**

| Phase | Duration | Intensity |
|---|---|---|

- Sleep <6 h the previous night, or two poor nights in a row
- Knee pain above 3/10 at rest
- Lower B on Thursday still producing significant soreness on Saturday
- Any week you are deloading

## 5. How hard, and when to move

There are no week numbers in this plan. Load moves when conditions are met.

### Add load only when ALL of these are true

- You hit the top of the rep range on **every** set at the target RIR
- The tempo held on the last rep of the last set
- The knee settled within 24 hours after the previous lower slot
- No tingling, numbness or facial symptoms in the last two sessions
- You slept 7 hours or more

Then: add the smallest available increment and drop back to the bottom of the rep range. That is double progression, and it is the whole progression model for everything except the tendon track.

### Back off when ANY of these are true

| Signal | Action |
|---|---|
| Knee pain >3/10, or not settled within 24 h | Drop the tendon load one tier |
| Any neurological symptom during or after the last session | Reduce load ~20%, remove overhead work entirely |
| Sleep under 6 h, or two poor nights running | Same loads, fewer sets |
| Resting HR up ≥7 bpm | Run Zone 2 instead of the 4×4 |
| Stalled at the same load twice running | Lighter cycle — see §6 |
| Joint or tendon soreness climbing across cycles | Lighter cycle. This is the main reason you would take one |
| Haematocrit or BP trending up | Pause the 4×4, contact your prescriber |

### The tendon track

Start the hack squat and leg extension at a load you can control for **about 15 reps at 3s/3s**. Over the months that drifts toward **6 reps** — but only when the 24-hour rule has cleared for **two consecutive sessions** at the current tier. If it hasn't, repeat the tier.

A tendon that takes six months to reach 6RM has still succeeded. One rushed there in eight weeks and flared has not. Rushing this is the single mistake that costs you the whole block.

### The plyometric gate

No jumping, bounding or reactive loading until **all three** are true: single-leg press ≈150% bodyweight pain-free on both legs; single-leg decline squat ≤2/10; four consecutive clear weeks with no 24-hour flare. Given the current neurological picture, clear it with your doctor before adding it even then.

## 6. Lighter cycles — triggered, not scheduled

**When:** any trigger in the back-off table above, or joint/tendon soreness climbing across cycles.

**What it looks like**
- **Volume cut ~40–50%** — roughly halve the sets
- **Effort at RIR 3–4**
- **Load and movement patterns kept** — do not go light and high-rep, that is a different stimulus
- **Zone 2 continues** as normal
- **4×4 skipped**, replaced with Zone 2

**Honest note.** Deloading is near-universal practice with thin direct support. A survey of 246 competitive athletes found deloads roughly every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks with volume cut and RIR raised — exactly this prescription, derived from practice rather than trials (Rogerson et al., *Sports Med Open* 2024;10:26). Coleman et al. (*PeerJ* 2024;12:e16777) tested a one-week deload and found **no** hypertrophy benefit.

**So why take them?** For the **tendon and joints**, not for muscle. Connective tissue adapts slower than muscle, and a symptomatic patellar tendon under twice-per-cycle heavy slow loading is the thing most likely to force an unplanned break. This is tendon insurance, and it is honest to call it that.

## 8. Integrated knee rehabilitation

### 8.1 First: confirm the diagnosis — this matters more than any exercise choice

This programme is built for **patellar tendinopathy**. Your brief specifies "bilateral knee rehabilitation" without naming a diagnosis, and the loading prescription is genuinely different across the plausible alternatives. Before Week 1, get this confirmed by a physiotherapist or sports physician.

| Presentation | Typical pattern | How the programme changes |
|---|---|---|
| **Patellar tendinopathy** | Localised pain at the inferior pole of the patella; pain with loading (jumping, squatting, decline); "warms up" then returns after; dose-dependent | **Programme as written.** HSR is the core. |
| **Quadriceps tendinopathy** | Pain at the **superior** pole of the patella | Same HSR logic, different palpation point. Broadly compatible. |
| **Patellofemoral pain** | Diffuse, retropatellar or peripatellar; worse with stairs, prolonged sitting, hills | Reduce deep-flexion loading early; **increase** hip abductor and quad strengthening; the deep hack squat may need range limits. |
| **Fat pad impingement** | Pain **below** the patella, worse with hyperextension and prolonged standing | Avoid terminal extension and end-range lockout. Leg extension may need range restriction. |
| **Early tibiofemoral OA** | Morning stiffness, crepitus, joint-line pain, swelling after volume | Volume and frequency become the tuning variables, not load. Different progression logic entirely. |

If you are 39, bilateral, and it developed gradually under lifting load, patellar tendinopathy is a reasonable prior — but "reasonable prior" is not "diagnosis," and a physiotherapist can settle it in one appointment.

### 8.2 The core: Heavy Slow Resistance

HSR is embedded in the lifting days, not run as a separate injury block:

| Day | Movement | Prescription |
|---|---|---|
| **Lower A** | Hack squat / pendulum squat | 4 × 6–10 at **3 s / 3 s**, progressing 15RM → 6RM across 12 weeks |
| **Lower A** | Seated leg extension (long-length) | 3 × 12–15 at **3 s / 3 s** |
| **Lower B** | Seated leg extension | 3 × 10–12 at **3 s / 3 s** |

That is the entire tendon programme. Two sessions per week, heavy, slow, progressive. Everything else on the lower days supports it.

**Why HSR:** never beaten by any other exercise intervention in the 2026 network meta-analysis (Liu 2026), and in Kongsgaard 2009 it matched eccentric training on pain and function at 12 weeks while producing superior collagen turnover, reduced neovascularisation and higher 6-month satisfaction. It also has the practical advantage of *being* strength training, so it does not compete with your other goals for time or recovery.

**Why the tempo:** 3 s up / 3 s down produces high time-under-tension at high load, which is the stimulus tendons appear to respond to. It also enforces control, which is what protects a symptomatic tendon during the descent.

### 8.3 The 24-hour rule — the governing constraint of this entire programme

- **Pain during loading: ≤3/10 is acceptable.** Tendon rehab is not meant to be pain-free.
- **Pain must return to baseline within 24 hours.**
- **Morning-after test: single-leg decline squat.** Same setup, same time of day, every morning after a lower day. Record 0–10.
- **If pain exceeds 3/10 during, or has not settled within 24 h:** drop the tendon load one tier at the next session. Do not push through.
- **If it flares twice in a row:** hold the tier for two weeks and get eyes on it from your physio.

This rule outranks the progression table, the RIR targets and your own impatience. It is the only real feedback signal a tendon gives you.

### 8.4 Supporting structures

Not optional — knee load is largely determined upstream and downstream of the knee.

| Structure | Movement | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Glute medius / frontal-plane hip control | Hip abduction | 2×/week |
| Adductors | Copenhagen adduction | 1–2×/week |
| Hamstrings | Seated leg curl + Nordic eccentric | 2×/week |
| Tibialis anterior | Tib raises | 2×/week |
| Gastrocnemius / soleus | Standing + seated calf raises | 2×/week each |
| Single-leg control | Bulgarian split squat, carries | 2×/week |

### 8.5 Isometrics — optional, honestly framed

**Spanish squat: 5 × 45 s at ~70% MVIC, 2 min rest.** Band anchored behind the knees, torso upright. Performed correctly this reaches roughly 70% MVIC where a wall sit typically reaches ~60%, which is what makes it a legitimate patellar tendon loader rather than just a burn.

The dosing is correctly transcribed from Rio 2015. The *rationale* is weaker than it is usually presented: **n = 6**, and the Clifford 2020 systematic review of 10 studies found isometric exercise was not superior to isotonic exercise for chronic tendinopathy at any timepoint up to 12 weeks.

**Use it as a personal experiment, not a mandate.** Try it before Lower A for two weeks. If the hack squat feels better afterwards, keep it. If it just makes your quads tired, move it to the end of the session as a finisher, or drop it. Either decision is evidence-consistent.

### 8.6 Treat the donut, not the hole

Tendon imaging abnormalities are common in asymptomatic people and correlate poorly with pain. Symptoms improve without structural change on imaging (van Ark et al. 2018). The tendon has a healthy, load-bearing region around the pathological area — the "donut" — and rehab works by building capacity in the donut, not by repairing the hole. This is why your progress metric is **what you can load without a 24-hour flare**, and never a scan.

### 8.7 Progression tests — run these every 4 weeks

| Test | Method | Target |
|---|---|---|
| **Single-leg decline squat** | 25° decline board, single leg, to ~60° knee flexion. Rate 0–10. | Trending down; ≤2/10 to unlock plyometrics |
| **Single-leg press** | Work up to a heavy 5, pain-free, both legs | **≈150% bodyweight** — the plyometric gate |
| **Left–right leg press asymmetry** | Compare single-leg 5RM | Within 10% |
| **Aerobic threshold** | 30 min at fixed HR, record distance/power | Improving every 8 weeks |

## 9. Recovery

**In order of leverage.**

1. **Sleep 7–9 h on a consistent schedule.** Biggest lever, no fabricated percentage attached (§2.9). Consistency of timing matters alongside duration.
2. **Protein 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day across 3–5 feedings.** At the upper end while running four lifting days plus tendon rehab.
3. **Adequate energy availability.** Your brief omits calories entirely, and it matters here: hypertrophy, four lifting days, two conditioning days and connective-tissue repair cannot all be funded from a deficit. If body composition is a goal, run it as a separate, later block — do not try to recomp through a tendon rehab phase.
4. **One full rest day**, Sunday, genuinely off.
5. **Deload every 6–8 weeks** (§6).
6. **Autoregulate with RIR.** The prescription is a target, not a contract. On a bad day, the same RIR at a lower load is the correct execution.
7. **Cardio and lifting fully separated** — the ≥6 h rule is satisfied automatically by the structure, permanently.

## 10. TRT-specific considerations

**What TRT does, supported:** restoring testosterone to high-normal increases muscle protein synthesis, satellite-cell activity, lean mass and strength, with dose-dependent effects within the physiological range (Bhasin et al. 1996, 2001). Combined with resistance training, the effects on lean mass and strength are additive.

**What TRT does not do:**
- It does **not** shorten the muscle protein synthesis window.
- It does **not** justify daily muscle hits or a higher training frequency than an evidence-based natural programme.
- It does **not** change the volume or proximity-to-failure landmarks.

**The volume-ceiling claim, honestly.** Your brief states TRT "raises the tolerable-volume ceiling → 16–20 sets/week for priority muscles is reasonable." I could not find direct evidence for this. The claim is a chain of plausible inferences — TRT improves recovery capacity → improved recovery permits more volume → more volume produces more hypertrophy — and each link is individually reasonable, but **no study has tested whether men on TRT have a higher optimal training volume than men not on TRT.** Rate it *mechanistically plausible, untested.* Operationally it barely matters: 16–20 fractional sets for priority muscles is well within the range this programme would prescribe anyway, and sits comfortably below the ~31-set PUOS from Pelland 2026. Run it, but attribute it to the volume literature, not to your prescription.

**Monitoring — non-negotiable, and your prescriber's call, not mine.**

| Marker | Why | Typical guidance |
|---|---|---|
| **Hematocrit** | The most common laboratory adverse effect of TRT. Elevated Hct raises blood viscosity. | Baseline, 3–6 months, then annually. **Endocrine Society (2018): withhold testosterone if Hct >54% until it normalises, then resume at a lower dose.** AUA (2018, validity confirmed 2024): **Hct ≥54% warrants intervention**; some clinicians use 50–52% as a watch threshold. Therapeutic phlebotomy is an accepted option. |
| **Blood pressure** | TRT and heavy resistance training can both raise BP; 4×4 intervals add acute pressure load. | Baseline and regularly. Home cuff readings are useful between labs. |
| **Lipids** | Formulation-dependent changes | Per prescriber |
| **PSA** | Standard TRT surveillance | Per prescriber |

**Why the 4×4 specifically needs clearing.** Maximal-HR intervals on top of TRT-driven erythrocytosis and heavy lifting stack three cardiovascular stressors. This is not a reason to avoid intervals — it is a reason to have your prescriber say yes explicitly, with a current hematocrit and BP in hand, before the first session. **If hematocrit is rising or trending toward the intervention threshold, pause the 4×4 and use Zone 2 until it is resolved.**

A relevant detail from the Mendelian randomisation work: **hematocrit and free testosterone in men were both causally associated with higher VO₂max** (Kjaergaard 2025). So on TRT, an improving VO₂max may partly reflect oxygen-carrying capacity rather than cardiac adaptation. Read the number alongside a hematocrit, not instead of one.

| 10 | Isometrics provide meaningful analgesia | **WEAK** | Rio 2015 is **n=6**. Clifford 2020 (10 studies): isometric **not superior** to isotonic at any timepoint ≤12 weeks. Optional tool, not a stage. |
| 11 | No exercise intervention beats HSR for patellar tendinopathy | **SUPPORTED** | Liu 2026 NMA, 17 RCTs, PMID 42192475. But rankings were exploratory and CIs wide. |
| 12 | Eccentric-only is "frequently weakest" | **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED / MISATTRIBUTED** | True in Li 2024 (*Heliyon*). In **Liu 2026, eccentric overload had the most favourable point estimate vs HSR** (SMD 0.33, CI −0.87 to 1.52, non-significant). Eccentric *step* training estimated less favourably. Do not merge the two findings. |
| 13 | Kongsgaard: HSR superior collagen turnover, less neovascularisation, higher satisfaction | **SUPPORTED** | Scand J Med Sci Sports 2009;19(6):790–802. **Add: tendon mechanical properties were unaffected by treatment.** Exact 70%/22% satisfaction figures not verifiable from the abstract. |
| 14 | Volume is the primary hypertrophy driver; frequency secondary | **SUPPORTED, refined** | Pelland 2026: 0.24%/set; PUOS ~31 fractional weekly sets. **Frequency shows consistent effects for strength but not hypertrophy** — a refinement, not a contradiction. |
| 15 | ~11 fractional sets per session ceiling | **SUPPORTED (preprint)** | Remmert 2025, SportRxiv — **not peer-reviewed**; high per-session volumes underrepresented. Soft ceiling. |
| 16 | 1–3 RIR captures nearly all hypertrophy | **SUPPORTED** | Refalo 2023; Robinson 2024. Strength comparatively insensitive to proximity to failure. |
| 17 | Zone 2 is optimal for mitochondrial adaptation | **CONTRADICTED** | Storoschuk 2025, *Sports Med* 55(7):1611–1624, PMID 40560504. Reframe Zone 2 around recovery cost and aerobic base. |
| 18 | VO₂max is among the strongest predictors of all-cause mortality | **SUPPORTED** | Lang 2024 (*BJSM*, >20.9M observations, 199 cohorts); Mandsager 2018; Kodama 2009. |
| 19 | Lundy 2026 shows 3.7% mortality reduction per 1 mL/kg/min | **MISATTRIBUTED** | Lundy 2026 is a cross-sectional survey of 340 marathoners with **wearable-estimated** VO₂max that **uses** the 3.7% figure as a modelling input. Cite Lang 2024 or Kodama 2009 instead. |
| 20 | VO₂max is not proven causally related to longevity | **SUPPORTED** | Kjaergaard 2025, *JCEM* 110(5):1451–1459 — genetically predicted VO₂max showed no association with longevity. **Add:** Generation 100 RCT (Stensvold 2020, *BMJ*) found no mortality difference over 5 years. |
| 21 | Helgerud 4×4 protocol and superiority over moderate training | **SUPPORTED** | *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 2007;39(4):665–671. **Add: the 15/15 protocol performed comparably** — intensity is the active ingredient, not the 4-minute block. |
| 22 | Long-muscle-length training produces greater hypertrophy | **SUPPORTED, modest** | Long vs short length ES 0.283 (CI 0.04–0.52). **But lengthened partials ≈ full ROM** (Wolf 2025). Select exercises at long lengths; don't abandon full ROM. |
| 23 | Collagen + vitamin C pre-loading increases collagen synthesis | **SUPPORTED (biomarker)** | Shaw 2017, n=8, propeptide outcome. **No strength benefit** (Kirmse 2024). |
| 24 | Deload every 6–8 weeks improves adaptation | **PRACTICE-BASED** | Survey-derived (Rogerson 2024: every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks). Coleman 2024 found no hypertrophy benefit. Justify deloads by tendon and joint management, not muscle growth. |

## 13. Thresholds that change the plan

| Signal | Action |
|---|---|
| **Any neurological symptom during a session** | Rack the weight, end the session. Note the load and whether you were breath-holding. Tell your doctor regardless. Next session: −20% load, no overhead work. |
| **Emergency signs** (spreading numbness, facial droop, slurred speech, weakness, balance loss, sudden severe headache, vision change) | **Stop and seek emergency care immediately.** |
| **Knee pain >3/10 during loading, or not settled within 24 h** | Drop tendon load one tier at the next session. Hold progression. |
| **Two consecutive 24-h flares** | Hold the tier for two weeks; get physiotherapist review. |
| **Knee pain at rest >3/10** | Swap Saturday's 4×4 for Zone 2. Reassess Lower A. |
| **Stalled lifts + poor sleep, or resting HR elevated ≥7 bpm** | Deload early. Do not wait for Week 7. |
| **Rising hematocrit, or approaching the intervention threshold** | **Pause the 4×4. Contact your prescriber.** Zone 2 only until resolved. |
| **Rising blood pressure** | Same — pause the 4×4, consult, continue lifting at reduced intensity pending advice. |
| **Aerobic threshold test stalled twice in a row** | Recovery budget is spent. Cut a lifting day's accessory volume by a third for two weeks, or take an extra rest day. |
| **Single-leg press ≥150% BW pain-free both legs + SLDS ≤2/10 + 4 clear weeks** | Unlock plyometric / energy-storage loading on Lower A. |
| **Explosive or athletic performance becomes a priority** | Add dedicated power work — and note this is the one goal where the interference effect genuinely bites. Keep 4×4 ≥48 h from power sessions. |
| **Session time consistently exceeding 90 min** | Cut the optional items first: neck, carries, Spanish squat, one lateral-raise set. Do not cut HSR or the compounds. |

## 14. Caveats

- **TRT management, hematocrit/BP/lipid/PSA monitoring, and clearance for maximal-HR intervals are medical decisions for your prescribing physician.** Nothing here substitutes for that.
- **Tendinopathy loading should be overseen by a physiotherapist**, and the diagnosis confirmed before Week 1 (§8.1). This programme assumes you can already squat, split-squat, lunge and leg-press with tolerable symptoms.
- **Isometric analgesia and "knee-friendly machine" claims rest on weaker evidence than the core progressive-loading principle.** Individualise both based on your own 24-hour symptom response.
- **Neck training has been removed entirely** given the cervical symptoms, and should not be reintroduced without medical clearance.
- **The per-session volume ceiling (~11 fractional sets) comes from a preprint.** Treat it as a reasonable heuristic, not settled science.
- **Where the original protocol was well-founded** — twice-weekly frequency, stretch-position exercise selection, removal of activation and pre-exhaust drills, gastroc/soleus differentiation, Spanish-squat dosing, HSR as the tendon core, collagen + vitamin C timing — **it has been preserved.** Only the overstated claims were corrected, and every correction is documented in §12.
- **The 4×4 requires medical clearance** before first use, and Zone 2 is always a valid substitute in slot 06.
- **Removed and not carried over:** the standalone half day; all neck training; standing overhead pressing; heavy shrugs; pre-exhaustion and activation supersets; redundant overlapping isolation angles; early ballistic knee loading; any "TRT justifies daily muscle hits" scheme; and the fabricated sleep-adaptation percentage.
